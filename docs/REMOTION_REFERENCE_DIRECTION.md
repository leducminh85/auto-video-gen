# Reference film direction

Observed source: `D:/Tool/genvideo 2/sample/sample-video.mp4`, 40.5 seconds, 1920×1080, 24 fps. The authored Remotion reference uses 60 fps; these are different files. Study sheets: `data/reference-study/first40.jpg`, `action.jpg` (4 frames/second), `dialogue.jpg` (2 frames/second).

## What the film actually does

- 0–4.5s: battery macro pulls out to an overhead workbench. Gloved hands, toast, coffee, tweezers and chips establish a working person and a physical place. The phone is the focal object; props support the activity.
- 4.5–7.5s: a cut reveals the opened phone. Tweezers bring the chip into contact, release, withdraw. A finger bends a contact. A small LED becomes active after contact. This is a sequence of state changes, not constant floating movement.
- 7.5–13s: elevator and garage demonstrate the consequence. Leo's working phone contrasts with surrounding people searching for reception. Their poses and eye lines explain the comparison. Inserts make the tiny phone display readable.
- 13–17s: seated tea action and title provide a pause in the rhythm.
- 18–31s: a bell curve builds progressively, populated by many silhouettes. IQ labels and a long camera travel communicate distance in the distribution. Gold is reserved for the important number. Do not use this chart for an unrelated script.
- 32–40.5s: kitchen geography is established once. Leaking pipe, bucket, companion, table and seated Leo remain consistent. Wide -> annoyed Leo -> diagnostic insert -> companion speaking -> Leo reply -> overhead repair note -> two-shot. Background blur isolates faces; alternating speaking turns and reactions carry the dialogue.

## Production rules derived from the reference

Write a causal story bible before drawings: recurring cast, goals, relationships, location geography, hero prop ownership and state. Every cut inherits the last action's result. Design a scene's entire shot sequence together. Never replace the location randomly to obtain variety.

For each action specify preparation, contact, consequence and settle. Use keyframes with holds. A walking character stops leg motion when arriving. Hand-held props use hand attachments. A prop changes ownership at pickup; do not show a second copy left on the table. A seated actor needs a chair and floor contact.

Establish spatial relationships, then cut close enough to read the mechanism or face. Use motivated tracking, push-in, overhead inserts and reaction shots. Keep screen direction and eye lines consistent. A closeup is deliberately restaged, not a full wide shot with every object squeezed smaller. Foreground, actors and background have different depth; blur the background only to focus on the action.

Effects must show an event: spark at electrical contact, ripple when a drop reaches water, impact when the tool touches, screen light after activation. Use quick onset and decay, then hold the resulting state. Do not sprinkle particles, glows, shakes or speed lines throughout the film.

Use warm illustrated environments, near-black silhouettes with readable cream faces, blue hoodie for Leo, scarf/glasses for the recurring companion, restrained gold emphasis, metallic tools and local shadows. Supporting actors have individual goals and timed reactions. Preserve the reference proportions rather than drawing emoji-like icons.

Review output for: meaningful cause/effect; readable focal action; consistent cast and prop state; exact speech coverage; alternation of framing; contact and ground placement; pauses around important events. Successful JSON validation or rendering alone is not visual approval.
